Some videos are not the thing you are doing but the thing next to it: a lecture
while you take notes, a match while you work, the second half of a film while a
download finishes. [Frame Player](/) has a mode for that. The window shrinks into
a corner of the screen, stays above every other window, and gets out of the
picture's way until you move the mouse over it.

## Using it

1. Open a video — a file, a link or a torrent, anything that plays.
2. Press **P**, or right-click the picture and choose **Mini player**. On a Mac
   it is also in the menu bar, under **View › Mini Player**, with ⌘⇧M.
3. The window shrinks to 420 wide, with a height that follows the picture's
   own shape, and goes to the bottom right corner of the screen — above the Dock
   or the taskbar, never under it. If the player was in full screen, it leaves
   full screen first.

**Moving it.** There is no title bar in the mini window, so you drag the picture
itself. Let go near an edge or a corner and the window glides the rest of the
way, to the same margin it opened at, so it never ends up a few pixels off the
edge. It snaps only once you have let go of the mouse button — pausing in the
middle of a drag does not pull it from under your hand. Whatever the snapping
setting, a mini window is never left hanging off the screen: with no title bar,
there would be nothing to grab it back by.

**Resizing it.** Drag an edge. The picture keeps its shape as the window grows
or shrinks, down to 240 by 135 pixels.

## What is in the small window

At that size, everything that belongs to a full-sized window would sit on
top of the picture, so most of it stands down:

| In the mini window | What happens to it |
|---|---|
| The seekbar and the times | Kept, with the hover preview, which shrinks to fit — or shows only the time when there is no room for a frame |
| Previous, play and next | Kept, in one row under the seekbar |
| Volume, speed, tracks, the other buttons | Hidden from the bar; the right-click menu is untouched |
| The title bar | Gone, and on a Mac the window buttons with it |
| A way out | A round button in the top right corner |

Everything fades when the mouse stops, as it does in full screen — the mini
player counts as full screen for the **Hide the controls** setting — and comes
back the moment you move over the window. Skipping an intro still works: the
skip button appears over the picture in a smaller size, and the on-screen
messages shrink to match.

On a Mac the mini window spends its life next to another app, so it handles
the click that brings it forward with care: that click activates the window and
does not pause the video. A pinch on the trackpad over it zooms into the picture
rather than resizing the window.

## Getting back

Press **P** again, click the **round button** in the corner, or choose
**Leave the mini player** in the right-click menu. The window goes back to the size
and the place it had before, and "always on top" goes back to whatever you had
it set to. A double click on the picture leaves the mini player and goes straight
to full screen (unless you set clicks on the picture to **Pause only**). Going
back to the start screen leaves the mini player too, because the button that
leaves it lives over the video.

## Over an app in full screen, on a Mac

This is the part that needed the most work, and the reason "always on top" is not
enough on its own.

On a Mac, an app in full screen is not a big window on your desktop — it is a
**space** of its own, the same kind of thing as a second desktop, and you swipe
between them. "Always on top" only lifts a window above the others on *its own*
space. So a plain always-on-top video window stays exactly where it was, on the
desktop you left, and disappears precisely when you go full screen in the app you
wanted to use it with.

The obvious fix is a higher level — put the window above everything. We tried
that, with a small test app that put labeled windows over a full-screen TextEdit
and took a picture of the screen, because reasoning about it had already been
wrong three times. **The level makes no difference.** An ordinary window does not
appear over another app's full-screen space at the floating level, at the level
of the menu bar, at the level of pop-up menus, or even at the very top level, above
everything the system itself draws. Allowing it on every space and marking it as
an auxiliary to full screen is needed but does not do it either.

What does it is the *kind* of window. macOS has a second kind besides the ordinary
one — a **panel**, the sort used for floating palettes and inspectors — and a
panel that does not make its app active when clicked is allowed onto another
app's full-screen space. Either half alone fails: a panel that activates its app
stays behind, and an ordinary window that promises not to activate stays behind
too. So while the mini player is on, Frame Player's window becomes such a panel,
and when you leave it, it becomes an ordinary window again, with every setting it
had before put back exactly — including the one that lets it go full screen at
all.

<Note>
  This is what lets the mini player follow you onto a full-screen browser,
  editor or presentation. It does not need any permission, and there is nothing
  to turn on.
</Note>

## Why it is not the system's Picture in Picture

IINA, the Mac's best-known mpv player, uses the system's own Picture in Picture,
and it is fair to ask why Frame Player does not. The answer is in how the player
is built. The video engine, mpv, draws into a view *inside* Frame Player's
window, and the whole interface — the seekbar, the menus, the subtitle panel —
is drawn on top of it. The system's Picture in Picture works by taking the video
out of the app and into a panel the system owns. Doing that here would mean
lifting mpv's view out of the window and leaving the interface behind, along
with the transparency the window depends on.

A small window of our own keeps all of that: the same seekbar with previews, the
same right-click menu, the same skip button. What it does not have is what only
the system's panel can do — tucking the video away into the side of the screen,
and resizing it with a pinch.

## On Windows

The mini player looks and behaves the same: the corner, the magnetic edges, the
controls that fade. Windows has no spaces for full-screen apps, so the floating
trick above is not needed there. The mini window is a topmost window, which is as
high as an ordinary window on Windows goes, and it stays above the windows you
work in. The exception is a game running in **exclusive** full screen, which takes
the display over completely; nothing on the desktop is drawn over that, our window
included.

## Always on top, without the mini player

If you want the ordinary window to stay above the others — a full-size window on a
second screen, say — that is a separate switch: right-click the picture and turn
on **Always on top** under **Window** (on a Mac it is also in the **View** menu).
It is remembered between launches. While the mini player is on, it is in charge
of keeping the window on top, and your own choice comes back when you leave it.
