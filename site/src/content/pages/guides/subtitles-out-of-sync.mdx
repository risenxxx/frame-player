There are two ways for subtitles to be out of sync, and they need opposite fixes.
One is a constant gap: every line is half a second late, from the first scene to
the last. The other grows: the first lines are fine, by the middle of the film
they are several seconds out, and by the end they are describing a different
scene. The first is a delay. The second is a difference in speed, and it is by
far the more common complaint about downloaded subtitles.

## Delay or drift: which one you have

Note how far out the subtitles are near the start, then skip ahead half an hour
and look again.

- **The gap is the same both times.** That is a delay. The subtitle delay setting
  in any player fixes it: set it once and the whole film is right.
- **The gap is bigger the second time.** That is drift. A delay setting cannot fix
  it, because it moves every line by the same amount while the error keeps
  growing.

## Where drift comes from

Films are shot at 24 frames per second. Two versions of that rate are in
circulation, and a third number shows up because of old European television:

- **23.976** (24000/1001) is how video made for North American television runs a
  film, and it is what most releases use today.
- **25** is what PAL television in Europe used. A film was simply played four per
  cent faster, so a two-hour film ran about five minutes shorter.
- **24** exactly is what cinemas use. Most Blu-ray films are 23.976 all the same.

A subtitle file is a list of times. Time it against a 25 fps copy and play it over
a 23.976 fps one, and every line after the first arrives a little early — four per
cent of the way into the film early.

| Into the film | 25 fps subtitles over a 23.976 fps video |
|---|---|
| 1 minute | 2.5 seconds early |
| 10 minutes | 25 seconds early |
| 30 minutes | 1 minute 14 seconds early |
| 60 minutes | 2 minutes 27 seconds early |
| 90 minutes | 3 minutes 41 seconds early |

Subtitles timed on a 23.976 fps copy and played over a 25 fps one go the other
way, later and later, by the same amounts. And between 23.976 and 24 the
difference is a tenth of a per cent: about three and a half seconds by the end of an hour, small enough to go
unnoticed until the final scene.

<Note>
  **A few subtitle formats count frames rather than time.** MicroDVD `.sub` files
  say "from frame 1204 to frame 1260", so they come out right or wrong depending on
  which frame rate the player assumes. Played at the wrong one they drift exactly
  like the table above.
</Note>

## How to fix drift

### The best fix: subtitles made for your copy

A subtitle that was timed against the same release as your video needs no fixing
at all. OpenSubtitles can find those by the file itself rather than by its name: it
computes a fingerprint from the file's size and the first and last 64 KB of it, and
returns the subtitles people uploaded for exactly that file. When there is no
match, look for a release name that matches yours and the same frame rate — most
subtitle sites list it next to each file.

### Convert the frame rate of the file

When there is no better subtitle, stretch the one you have. It is a one-time edit
that fixes the file for good:

- **Subtitle Edit** (Windows, free): *Synchronization › Change frame rate*, from 25
  to 23.976 — or the other way round if the subtitles fall behind. Its *Point sync*
  does the same from two lines you match by hand, which also fixes a delay at the
  same time.

### Or stretch it while you watch

- **mpv** can stretch subtitle times on the fly with `--sub-speed`. For 25 fps
  subtitles over a 23.976 fps video that is `--sub-speed=25/23.976`.
- **VLC** has a subtitle speed setting under *Tools › Track Synchronization*.

These last only for the session (or for every file, if you put them in a
configuration file), which is why converting the file is usually the better fix.

## How Frame Player helps

  The subtitle search in [Frame Player](/): the first result was found by the file

- **It searches by the file first.** The subtitle panel asks OpenSubtitles for
  subtitles made for your exact copy, and says whether a result came from that
  match or from a search by title — because a title match may be a different
  release, and the timing is then worth checking.
- **A different frame rate is flagged before you download.** When a result was
  made for a frame rate other than your video's, the panel marks it, so the one
  that is going to drift is not the one you pick.
- **It offers to fit the subtitles to your video.** Download one made for a
  different frame rate and the panel asks whether to stretch it to match. It asks
  rather than doing it, because the frame rate on a subtitle site is whatever the
  uploader typed.
- **The subtitle menu can stretch any subtitle by hand**: 25 → 23.976,
  23.976 → 25 or 24 → 23.976. That covers a file you already had and a track
  inside the video, where nobody said which frame rate it was made for. It works
  on text subtitles; picture-based ones (PGS, VobSub) cannot be stretched.
- **The speed and the delay you set are kept for that file**, and only that file,
  so the subtitles stay fixed the next time you open it and nothing leaks into
  the next episode.
- **Subtitles inside a torrent are attached by themselves**, matched to the
  episode you are playing.

Fix the speed first, then use the delay for whatever gap is left. If you want the
subtitle file itself fixed for every player, converting it in Subtitle Edit still
does that for good.
