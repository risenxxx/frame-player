Put the same film in QuickTime and in IINA, mpv or Frame Player on a Mac, and the
mpv-based ones look darker: faces in a dim room lose their shape, a night scene
turns into a black field with lights in it. The usual explanations are that mpv
"crushes the shadows", or that the file has no colour information and mpv guesses
wrong. We measured it, and neither is quite what happens.

## What the difference actually is

The test was one frame of H.264 video with sixteen grey bars of known brightness,
played in QuickTime and in Frame Player on the same MacBook Pro display, both
windows captured and read pixel by pixel. The numbers are what each player sent
to the screen, out of 255:

| Brightness in the file (16 = black, 235 = white) | QuickTime | mpv's curve | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 3.7 | 4.3 | +0.6 |
| 28 | 11.0 | 13.6 | +2.6 |
| 40 | 29.7 | 27.6 | −2.1 |
| 64 | 63.3 | 55.5 | −7.8 |
| 96 | 102.3 | 91.6 | −10.7 |
| 128 (mid-grey) | 140.0 | 128.7 | −11.3 |
| 160 | 176.7 | 167.1 | −9.6 |
| 224 | 242.7 | 240.6 | −2.1 |

Two things stand out. The very darkest steps are not darker in mpv at all — they
are a level or two lighter. What mpv draws darker is **the lower mid-tones**, and
by up to 12 levels. That is where most of a dim scene lives, which is why it reads
as lost shadow detail.

On real footage the gap is the same shape: on a bright animated frame the whole
picture averaged 177.5 in QuickTime and 170.9 in mpv's curve, and the pixels in
the file's own shadows 54.6 against 47.9 — about an eighth darker.

The colours themselves are identical. Pure red came out as 232, 51, 35 in QuickTime
and 231, 50, 34 in Frame Player. This is a tone curve, not a colour shift.

<Note>
  **IINA draws what mpv draws — on a file with no colour tags.** On the untagged
  frame IINA matched Frame Player's default to within half a level on every bar.
  On the same frame tagged as BT.709 it drew darker still: mid-grey at 122, and
  the step that QuickTime shows at 11 at 3. That one really is shadows going
  black, and it is the tagged file that does it.
</Note>

## Why: two ways to read BT.709

Almost every SDR video — films, series, YouTube — is BT.709. The standard says how
the picture is *encoded*; how a screen should turn it back into light is a separate
standard, BT.1886, and it describes a curve of roughly 2.4 for a dim room, the room
a colourist grades in.

- **mpv** hands the screen the video's values unchanged. The Mac's display then
  reads them with its own curve, close to 2.2 — a little lighter than the 2.4 of
  the reference, but the same family.
- **QuickTime** decodes BT.709 with a curve of about **1.96** and then
  encodes the result for the display. At mid-grey that is 140 instead of 129. It
  is a long-standing Apple choice, discussed among video editors as the
  "QuickTime gamma shift", and it fits our measurements closely: the model
  predicts 63.6 at the 64 bar and 141 at mid-grey; QuickTime showed 63.3 and 140.

So by the standard, mpv's darker picture is the closer one. By habit, on a Mac,
QuickTime's lighter one is the familiar one: it is what the system's own player
shows — and, by the reports that prompted this test, what other players built
on Apple's video framework show too — and a video edited and checked on a Mac
was often signed off looking like that. Neither is a bug, and neither is universally right.

## "The file has no colour information"

Many reports of this involve MKV files where `ffprobe` shows no `color_space` and
no `color_range`, so it is natural to suspect that the player guesses badly. We
stripped every colour tag from the test video, and then made a copy tagged
properly as BT.709. Both players drew the tagged and the untagged copy
**identically**, bar for bar. mpv assumes BT.709 for an untagged HD video, and so
does QuickTime; they then draw BT.709 each in their own way. Adding tags to your
files will not change the picture in either. IINA was the exception: there the
tagged copy came out darker than the untagged one, as the note above says.

## What a colour-bar test measures

Another way these players get compared is SMPTE colour bars: generate them in
BT.709, read each bar off the screen with Digital Color Meter in "Display in
sRGB" mode, and average how far it lands from the "theoretical" value. We ran
that test too. Its theoretical values are the video's own code values sent to
an sRGB display untouched — which is exactly mpv's curve. So Frame Player's
default came out 0.2 levels off on average, QuickTime 3.5, and our "As in
macOS" setting 3.6, precisely because it matches QuickTime. The score tells you
which convention a player follows, not whether it is accurate.

It also hides most of the difference. Seven of the 27 patches are pure black
or full white, where every curve agrees; most of the rest are 75 % colours,
near the top of the curve; only two are mid-tones. Between QuickTime and mpv
the bars differ by 3.6 levels on average, while a plain grey ladder shows 8.3
in the mid-tones — more than twice as much, in the part of the picture where
a dim scene actually lives. If you want to see this difference, look at greys,
not at colour bars. (And if you compare your numbers with ours: our window
captures read within one level of Digital Color Meter in "Display in sRGB"
mode on the same frame.)

## The recipe that goes around, and why it did not work for us

The fix usually passed along for IINA is three mpv settings:

```
gamma-factor=1.1218765935747068
vf=format=gamma=gamma2.2
icc-profile-auto=yes
```

The first two are the heart of it: they tell mpv to treat the video as a plain 2.2
curve and then bend it by 2.2 / 1.961 — Apple's 1.96. In Frame Player the tone
curve came out right with them. But the third, the display's ICC profile, made
every colour paler: pure red went from 232, 51, 35 to 213, 68, 49, green from 117,
251, 76 to 152, 244, 103. The picture was being colour-managed twice — once by mpv
for the display, and once more by macOS, which already converts Frame Player's
video layer for the screen on its own.

Without the ICC line, the curve alone lifted the deepest shadows too far (the 28
bar at 18.7 instead of QuickTime's 11.0), because mpv then encodes for a pure 2.2
display while the Mac's display curve has a straight section near black. What
finally matched was a fourth setting that makes mpv use that real curve:

```
target-trc=srgb
treat-srgb-as-power22=no
```

With the format filter and the gamma factor, that reproduced QuickTime to within
half a level from the 32 bar up, and to within two units on every colour.

<Note>
  **These are the settings that matched in Frame Player.** How a player hands its
  picture to macOS decides whether ICC belongs in the recipe, and we did not
  measure IINA's or mpv's own window with them. If you try them elsewhere, compare
  against QuickTime before keeping them — and apply them to SDR files only, since
  the format filter would mislabel an HDR video.
</Note>

## How Frame Player handles it

Frame Player keeps mpv's curve by default, and offers QuickTime's as a choice:
**Settings › Video › SDR video color › As in macOS**.

- **It is the combination measured above**, without ICC, so the colours stay
  exactly as they were and only the tone curve changes.
- **It changes the picture at once**, even on a paused frame — flip it back and
  forth to see which you prefer.
- **It touches SDR video only.** HDR files keep their own tone mapping, and images
  are left alone. When an HDR file follows an SDR one, the settings are back
  before its first frame is drawn.
- **Your own mpv.conf wins.** If you set `gamma-factor` or `target-trc` there
  yourself, the switch leaves that line alone.
- **Screenshots follow the choice**, so a frame you export looks the way the
  player showed it.

The switch is on macOS only: the question it answers is "mpv or Apple", and on
Windows there is no Apple side to match.
