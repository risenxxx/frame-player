Going through a video one frame at a time is how an animator checks timing, how
a coach finds the exact moment a foot leaves the ground, how a reviewer marks a
cut, and how anyone gets the one sharp frame worth saving. Forward is easy. Back
is where most players stall: press the key and wait, press again and wait again.
That is not a bug in any one player. It follows from how video is compressed —
and it can be worked around.

## Why a step back costs more than a step forward

Almost no frame in a modern video file is stored whole. Every so often there is
a **keyframe**, a complete picture; every frame after it, until the next
keyframe, is stored only as the difference from the frames around it. The
keyframe and the frames that depend on it are called a group of pictures, and a
group can be anything from half a second to ten seconds long.

So the decoder can only ever go forward from a keyframe. To show the next frame
it applies one more difference. To show the previous one, it has nothing to
apply the difference *backward* to — it has to go back to the keyframe and decode
forward again, up to the frame before the one on screen:

| To show | What the decoder has to do |
|---|---|
| The next frame | Decode one frame |
| The previous frame, just after a keyframe | Decode a handful of frames from that keyframe |
| The previous frame, at the end of a 10-second group at 60 fps | Decode the group again from its keyframe — up to 600 frames, to show one |

That is why the cost of a step back depends on the **file**, not on the player:
a film from a disc with two-second groups is cheap to step through, and the
long-group files that video sites hand out are expensive. And a player that
does nothing about it pays the full cost again at every single press.

## What a step back costs, measured

mpv — the engine inside Frame Player, IINA, mpv.net and a few others — steps
back exactly that way: each press is an exact seek to the previous frame, so a
decode from the keyframe. We timed it on five test files, with the decoder in
software and in the Mac's hardware decoder:

| One step back | Software decoding | Hardware decoding |
|---|---|---|
| 1080p, 24 fps, H.264, 2-second groups | 60 ms | 190 ms |
| 1080p, 60 fps, H.264, 10-second groups | 160 ms | 1.9 s |
| 1080p, 60 fps, VP9, 5-second groups | 260 ms | 1.3 s |
| 1080p, 60 fps, AV1, 10-second groups | 1.0 s | 2.3 s |
| 4K HEVC, 10-second groups | 1.6 s | 1.8 s |

<p class="table-note">Measured with mpv 0.41 on an Apple Silicon Mac (M3 Max), on generated 60-second files, so the absolute numbers are optimistic and the ratios are what carry over. A step forward costs one frame period in every case: 17 ms at 60 fps, 42 ms at 24.</p>

The second column is the surprise. **Hardware decoding makes a step back two to
ten times slower**, even though it is faster for watching. A hardware decoder is
built to stream frames out steadily, and it hands them back with a latency that
a seek of one frame cannot hide. Most players, and most mpv setups, play with
hardware decoding on — so the slowest case is the usual one.

## What Frame Player does about it

Two things, each measured before it shipped, and each switched on only where it
helps.

### 1. Software decoding while you step

The first step back of a session moves the decoder from the graphics hardware to
software. The switch itself takes about 10 ms, and the very next step already
runs at software speed. On a real 4K HEVC release with two-second groups, that
alone took a step back from 230–410 ms down to 130–220 ms.

The hardware comes back when you press play — and **only while the video is
still paused**, which is the part that took the longest to get right. A decoder
switched during playback is fed the data already on its way, from the middle of
a group, without the keyframe it depends on. A hardware AV1 or VP9 decoder fails
on that, and mpv then quietly stays in software for the rest of the file. So
the play button, the space bar, a click on the picture and the media keys all
wait for the hardware to be back before the video moves.

That wait is a real cost: 0.4–0.6 s on the VP9 file, 0.7–0.8 s on an H.264 film
and 1.4–1.6 s on the AV1 file, against 5–50 ms for an ordinary resume. We measured the alternative — simply playing on in software — and it is
worse for longer:

| Playing on after stepping | Software | Hardware |
|---|---|---|
| H.264 film | 25 % of a core | 10 % |
| 1080p, 60 fps, VP9 | 33–42 % | 8–12 % |
| 1080p, 60 fps, AV1 | 46 % | 9–11 % |
| 1440p, 60 fps, VP9 | 45 % | 11 % |

<p class="table-note">Same machine, no frames dropped in either. The power the system reports follows the same ratios.</p>

Three to four times the power for the rest of the film, to save a second once,
is not a trade worth making on a laptop.

### 2. Decoding backward, once, on files where a step is still slow

Software decoding fixes a film with short groups. It does not fix a file whose
groups are ten seconds long, where even software needs a second per press. For
those, Frame Player switches mpv into **backward decoding**: mpv decodes the
whole group once, keeps the frames, and hands them out in reverse. The first
press pays for the group; every press after it costs one frame period, like a
step forward.

Turning that on and off has its own cost, so the player is careful about when:

- **Only on a file that has shown itself slow.** Any step back that takes longer
  than 100 ms marks the file, and no later fast step clears the mark — a step
  just after a keyframe is always quick and says nothing about the next one.
- **Only from the second step back in a row.** A single step back, or steps that
  alternate with steps forward, stay the ordinary kind: turning around costs
  more than one ordinary step, and paying it at every change of direction would
  make rocking back and forth slower, not faster.
- **Only inside a memory budget.** Holding a decoded group is expensive — a
  ten-second group of 8-bit 4K at 24 fps is 3 GB, and we measured mpv's
  footprint at exactly what the arithmetic predicts. Before turning, the player
  measures how long the groups around your position are (it reads only the
  packet headers, which takes milliseconds) and how much memory the computer
  has, and goes ahead only if the longest group fits in a quarter of it, capped
  at 4 GB. Otherwise it keeps stepping the ordinary way.
- **With the sound off.** mpv would otherwise decode the audio backward too. On a
  4K release with a 5.1 soundtrack that tripled the wait to get started (2.0 s
  against 0.7 s) and made one step in forty skip five frames. The track comes
  back when you play — and only after the decoder has turned forward again,
  because a track switched on while decoding backward lands on the next
  keyframe instead of the current frame.

<Note>
  **Every landing is checked.** When the decoded group does not fit, mpv does
  not report an error — it drops frames and the picture lands seconds away from
  where it should (we watched a 4K file jump from 39.9 s to 33.5 s). So after
  every step the player compares where it landed with where one frame back
  should be. If they differ, it turns forward, seeks to the right frame and
  steps that file the ordinary way from then on.
</Note>

The result, on the 4K HEVC release with a 5.1 soundtrack: 0.69 s to get started,
then 37–48 ms a step, about the same as a step forward — with one step of
340 ms where the walk crosses into the previous group. Pressing play resumes
from the frame you stopped on, with the soundtrack and hardware decoding back.

### And everything else has to know

A player that decodes backward has to make sure nothing is left that way. A seek
from the seekbar or the keys first turns mpv forward and gives the decoder back,
because mpv lets a pending seek replace another and the viewer's seek would
otherwise be the one lost; anything else that moves the picture — a chapter, a
watch-together room — is noticed the moment it lands, and mpv is turned forward at once. And all of these settings are
made for the current file only, so mpv itself resets them when the next one
opens: the next episode can never start out playing backward from its last
frame.

## Torrents

Backward decoding runs only on a file that is on this computer's disk, so it
covers local files and torrents — including one still downloading, where it
needs more care. A part of
a torrent that has not arrived yet does not fail when read: it reads back as
zeros. So on a torrent still downloading, the player checks its map of what has
arrived before every step backward, and uses backward decoding only where that
covers the groups being decoded. What you have just watched has always arrived;
the gap is the stretch before a jump ahead into what had not. There the player
steps the ordinary way, and if a step has to wait for the swarm, it says so
rather than appearing to hang. Online streams always step the ordinary way.

## Using it

- **Comma** steps one frame back and **period** one frame forward, on both
  systems; hold either to keep going. Both can be rebound in the shortcut editor.
- **S** saves the frame on screen, **Shift+S** saves it with the subtitles, and
  **Ctrl+C** (**⌘C** on a Mac) copies it to the clipboard. The frame is saved as
  the player decoded it, HDR tone mapping included.
- Backward decoding can be switched off under **Settings › Video › Fast stepping
  back by frame**; software decoding while stepping has no switch, since it
  changes nothing but the speed.
- While casting to a television there is nothing on the computer to step through,
  so the player says frame stepping is not available until the cast ends.

The numbers on this page were measured on a Mac. On Windows the player does the
same things — software decoding while stepping, backward decoding inside the
same memory budget — with the graphics card's own decoder instead of Apple's,
and we have not timed it there.
