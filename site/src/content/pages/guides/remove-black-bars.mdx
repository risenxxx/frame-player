You open a film, make it full screen, and there is black above and below it — or,
on a wide monitor, a black frame all the way round. Changing the aspect ratio
squashes everything, zooming cuts off the sides, and a different copy of the same
film had no bars at all. The bars are not the player's doing: they are pixels in
the video, and they have to be cropped off. [Frame Player](/) can measure where
they are and remove exactly those.

## Bars the player adds, and bars that are in the file

There are two kinds of black bar, and only one of them can be removed.

**Bars the player adds** appear whenever the picture and the screen have
different shapes. A film made at 2.39:1, the usual cinema widescreen, is wider
than a 16:9 screen, so it is shown full width with black above and below. That is
the whole picture, as large as it fits. Nothing removes those bars without
cutting picture off.

**Bars inside the file** were put there when the video was made. A Blu-ray is
1920×1080, a 16:9 frame, so a 2.39:1 film on it is stored with black strips above
and below the picture — *letterboxed*. A 4:3 television series remastered into a
16:9 frame gets strips at the sides — *pillarboxed*. And a 4:3 programme inside a
letterboxed 16:9 frame has them on all four sides. To the player, those strips are
picture: black picture, but part of the frame it was given. That is also why two
copies of one film can differ — one stored at the full 16:9 with the bars, one
already cropped to the film's own shape.

## Why they show up on all four sides

On a 16:9 screen, a letterboxed 16:9 file looks the same as a cropped one: bars
above and below either way. The bars in the file start to show when the screen,
or the window, has another shape.

| What you are watching | Where | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| 2.39:1 film stored in a 16:9 file | Ultrawide 21:9 monitor | The 16:9 frame is pillarboxed by the player, and the film inside it is letterboxed by the file: a small picture with black on all four sides |
| The same file | A window | The window has the frame's 16:9 shape and the film fills only the middle of it |
| 4:3 series stored in a 16:9 file | A MacBook's 16:10 screen | Black at the sides from the file, and thin black above and below from the screen |
| A cropped copy of the same film | Any of these | Bars only where the screen's shape needs them |

Cropping the file's bars turns the first kind of copy into the last.

## How other players do it

**By eye.** VLC's *Video › Crop* menu offers a list of fixed ratios, and most
players have something similar. You pick the one that looks right, it cuts a
centered rectangle of that shape, and you pick it again for the next file.

**By watching the screen.** mpv has no crop button, but it can run FFmpeg's
`cropdetect` filter, which reports the bars in the frames that are playing, and
mpv's source tree carries a script, `autocrop.lua`, that runs it on the scene in
front of you and crops to what it found. The trouble is the scene. A night shot
has dark edges that are picture, an opening can be black for its first minute, and
a scene framed with darkness at the top looks exactly like a letterbox to anything
that sees only that scene. The filter also needs the frames in ordinary memory, so
with hardware decoding it adds a copy of every frame back from the graphics card
while it runs.

## How Frame Player does it

Open the picture panel — right-click the video and choose **Picture**, or press
**C** — and pick one of the shapes under **Crop**: **None**, **Auto**, **2.39**,
**1.85** or **4:3**.

### Auto measures the whole file, not the scene on screen

Auto opens the file a second time, separately from playback, and decodes eight
keyframes spread from 10 % to 90 % of its length. On each it walks in from every
edge, line by line, until it meets picture: a line counts as bar while no more
than one sample in fifty is more than 16 levels above black, so the faint noise
compression leaves in a bar, or a single hot pixel, does not end it.

Then the frames vote, edge by edge, and each edge keeps the **smallest** bar it was
shown. A dark scene reports bars that are too big; a bright one reports the real
ones; the smallest is the right one. One frame is allowed to disagree, so a
subtitle burnt into the bottom strip, or a logo in a corner, does not veto the
crop for the whole film. Two frames reaching an edge mean the edge is picture.

The rest is caution:

- Strips under 1 % of the frame are left alone — that is encoder padding, and
  cropping it would change the window's shape for nothing anyone can see.
- A crop that would keep less than 30 % of the width or height is treated as a
  misreading, and nothing is cropped.
- Fewer than three frames to measure, as in a short clip that is dark throughout,
  and the answer is "No black bars found".

We measured it on letterboxed and pillarboxed test files, 10-bit HEVC among them,
and on footage made to fool it — dark fireworks, space scenes: the rectangle was
exact every time, five full-frame films came back with nothing to crop, and a 4K
HEVC file took under a second.

### The named shapes

2.39 and 1.85 are the two ratios cinema films are letterboxed to; 4:3 is the one
television programmes are pillarboxed to. Choosing one cuts a centered rectangle
of that shape, worked out from the frame's displayed shape, so an anamorphic file,
an aspect ratio you set by hand and a rotated video all come out right. If the
picture already has that shape, the player says so and leaves it. Use these when
you know the ratio, when the bars are not black enough to measure, or on a link,
where Auto is not offered.

### One choice for the season, a measurement for each episode

Picking a crop on one episode records it for the folder, or for the torrent,
and the panel says so: *For the whole playlist. "Auto" looks for bars in each
file separately.* When the next episode opens it applies the choice to itself —
Auto measures that episode, a named shape is computed from its frame. A rectangle
is never copied from one file to another, because episodes of one release usually
share their bars but not always, and a finale in a wider ratio cut to the pilot's
rectangle would lose picture.

Each file remembers the rectangle it came to, including "no bars here", so
reopening it costs nothing. Choosing **None** turns the crop off for the playlist
as well.

### On a torrent that is still downloading

A torrent's pieces are on your disk, so Auto works there too — but a file still
arriving has holes, and a frame from a hole is not a frame. So the player looks
only inside the stretches already downloaded, usually the start and the part around
where you are watching, and spreads its samples over those. Fewer scenes can only
make it crop less, never more. We checked that on copies with the middle half of
the file blanked out: samples from the ends found the exact rectangle, and even
samples placed inside the blank gave no wrong answer.

If too little has arrived to measure three different places, the panel says
*Too little is downloaded yet, try again shortly*. When an episode opens with Auto
already chosen, the player tries again by itself while it plays, every 30
seconds, four times in all.

## The window takes the picture's shape

With **Window to video** on **Aspect**, which is the default, the window has the
shape of the picture — and after a crop, that is the shape of the film rather than
of the frame it was stored in. A letterboxed film opens into a wide window with
no black in it, and the window keeps that shape while you resize it. The seekbar's
hover previews follow too: they show the cropped picture, not the frame with its
bars.

## Cropping is not "Fill the screen"

The picture panel has one more control that gets rid of black bars, and it does
something different.

**Crop** removes the bars that are in the file and keeps all of the picture. Use
it on any file that has them.

**Fill the screen** enlarges the picture until there are no bars left at all, and
cuts off whatever no longer fits — the sides of a 2.39:1 film on a 16:9 screen, the
top and bottom of a 4:3 one. That is picture, not bars: titles, faces at the edge
of the frame, subtitles burnt into the image.

<Note>
  **They work together.** Crop first, so the file's own bars are gone and the
  picture is as large as it can be without losing anything. If you still want a
  screen with no black at all, turn on Fill the screen as well, knowing that from
  there on, what goes is the film.
</Note>

The panel also has brightness, contrast, saturation and gamma, and on a Mac the
choice between [mpv's and QuickTime's tone curves](/guides/darker-than-quicktime)
is in the settings. For a soft, low-resolution source,
[neural-network upscaling](/upscaling) is in the same panel.
